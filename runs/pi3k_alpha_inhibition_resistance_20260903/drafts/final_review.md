# Resistance to PI3K-alpha Inhibition: Clinical Boundaries, Pathway Reactivation, and Biomarker-Guided Next Steps

## Abstract

PI3K-alpha inhibitors have converted PIK3CA mutation from a descriptive genomic alteration into a therapeutic entry point, most clearly in hormone receptor-positive, HER2-negative advanced breast cancer. Resistance, however, is not explained by PIK3CA status alone. Across randomized trials, translational cohorts, rapid-autopsy studies, cell-line models, xenografts, organoids, and biomarker reviews, resistance emerges from overlapping processes: incomplete or interrupted drug exposure, endocrine-partner escape, PTEN loss and p110-beta compensation, secondary PIK3CA mutations, AKT or mTOR output persistence, receptor tyrosine kinase and MAPK bypass, and lineage- or state-dependent signaling rewiring. The evidence is strongest where clinical progression samples are paired with functional rescue experiments; it is weaker where mechanisms are inferred from reviews, single models, or pathway markers without treatment-timing information. This review separates eligibility biomarkers from resistance mechanisms, direct clinical evolution from model-supported hypotheses, and actionable biomarker claims from exploratory associations. A practical framework for PI3K-alpha inhibitor resistance should therefore ask when the sample was taken, which drug and partner therapy were used, which assay measured the mechanism, and whether the proposed intervention is supported by patient evidence, model evidence, or both.

## Main Review

### 1. PIK3CA Mutation Is an Entry Point, Not a Resistance Explanation

The modern clinical story begins with alpelisib plus fulvestrant. In the FDA approval summary of SOLAR-1, patients with hormone receptor-positive, HER2-negative advanced or metastatic breast cancer and PIK3CA-mutated tumors had longer median progression-free survival with alpelisib plus fulvestrant than with placebo plus fulvestrant, establishing PIK3CA mutation as a treatment-selection biomarker for that regimen [1]. That evidence does not mean that every PIK3CA-mutant cancer is uniformly PI3K-alpha dependent, nor that progression on therapy can be interpreted as a single pathway event. PIK3CA status identifies a population in which PI3K-alpha inhibition can help; resistance requires additional information about tumor state, drug exposure, partner therapy, and evolution during treatment.

The biomarker boundary is widened, but not erased, by broader genomic profiling. Large tissue and liquid-biopsy analyses show that many breast cancers carry pathogenic PIK3CA alterations outside the original SOLAR-1 hotspot set, and retrospective real-world data suggest that some non-SOLAR-1 pathogenic mutations may still be clinically targetable with alpelisib plus fulvestrant [2]. Assay design also matters: targeted NGS and RT-PCR can both support PIK3CA testing, but they differ in variant breadth, tissue requirements, and sensitivity to specimen quality [3]. Thus, a negative or narrow hotspot result may reflect the test as well as the tumor, whereas a positive result remains a therapeutic eligibility signal rather than a mechanistic explanation for later failure.

Clinical outcomes after PI3K-alpha inhibitor initiation reinforce this separation. Alpelisib combinations outside the pivotal endocrine doublet, real-world post-CDK4/6 series, and subgroup analyses all show that benefit varies with patient selection, line of therapy, comorbidity, partner treatment, and toxicity management [4-7]. In the German PRAEGNANT experience, outcomes after prior CDK4/6 inhibitor exposure appeared shorter than pivotal-trial expectations in some contexts, with small subgroups and confounding clinical features limiting inference [6]. These studies are useful because they keep resistance grounded in clinical practice: a tumor may progress because the pathway rewires, because the endocrine component fails, because exposure is interrupted, or because the selected assay only partly captured the disease.

### 2. Exposure and Therapeutic Index Shape Apparent Resistance

For PI3K-alpha inhibitors, resistance is not purely molecular. Alpelisib is clinically effective but metabolically costly; hyperglycemia and other adverse events can force dose interruptions, reductions, or discontinuation, which complicates any claim that progression reflects intrinsic tumor escape [1,10-12]. Hyperglycemia analyses and management studies emphasize baseline metabolic risk, early monitoring, and prophylactic or reactive interventions, including metformin-based strategies, as practical determinants of sustained exposure [10-12]. These papers do not prove that metabolic toxicity creates molecular resistance, but they do show that pharmacology and tolerability can determine whether a susceptible tumor receives enough continuous pathway pressure.

Inavolisib was developed partly to improve the balance between mutant pathway targeting and tolerability. Early phase data and mechanistic studies describe a molecule with mutant PI3K-alpha degradation activity and combinations with endocrine therapy and CDK4/6 inhibition [13-17]. INAVO120 then placed inavolisib with palbociclib and fulvestrant in PIK3CA-mutated endocrine-resistant advanced breast cancer, reporting survival and safety outcomes in a regimen and treatment-line context distinct from alpelisib plus fulvestrant [13-15]. The important interpretation is not that inavolisib eliminates resistance, but that drug design, partner regimen, and sequence can change the selective pressure under which resistance evolves.

A related theme is vertical or class-shift targeting. Allosteric and mutant-selective PI3K-alpha inhibitors such as STX-478 and RLY-2608 were designed to spare wild-type PI3K-alpha and reduce metabolic liabilities while maintaining antitumor activity in PIK3CA-mutant models [20-22]. The most direct acquired-resistance evidence comes from work showing that secondary PIK3CA mutations can alter orthosteric inhibitor binding and that some modeled resistance mutations retain sensitivity to allosteric PI3K-alpha inhibition [19]. That is promising but narrow. It supports a class-switch hypothesis for particular on-target resistance alleles; it does not yet prove that all patients progressing on orthosteric PI3K-alpha inhibitors should switch to an allosteric inhibitor, especially when AKT1, PTEN, RTK, MAPK, endocrine, and exposure-related mechanisms can coexist.

### 3. PTEN Loss and p110-beta Compensation Are Central but Not Universal

PTEN loss is among the clearest resistance mechanisms because it connects patient progression, spatial heterogeneity, and functional rescue. In a rapid-autopsy case of PIK3CA-mutant metastatic breast cancer treated with BYL719, progressing metastases showed convergent PTEN loss, and experimental PTEN knockdown restored downstream signaling despite p110-alpha inhibition. Sensitivity could be restored by combining p110-alpha and p110-beta blockade, and PTEN-null patient-derived xenograft models were resistant to BYL719 but responsive to broader or combined PI3K inhibition [23]. This is unusually strong mechanistic evidence, but it remains anchored in a small number of deeply studied cases and models rather than a population-level prevalence estimate.

Clinical cohort data broaden the picture. In a phase I/II study of alpelisib plus aromatase inhibition, paired tissue and ctDNA profiling found PTEN alterations in resistant cases, including baseline loss-of-function events in rapidly progressing patients and acquired events after therapy [8]. The same study showed ESR1 mutation expansion, illustrating a central problem for combination therapy: progression can reflect PI3K pathway escape, endocrine-partner escape, or both. A later cohort of patients progressing on alpelisib or inavolisib found acquired PI3K pathway alterations in about half of serial ctDNA cases, including PTEN loss-of-function alterations, but also AKT1, secondary PIK3CA, FGFR, KRAS, and BRAF changes [19]. PTEN loss is therefore important, but it is one member of a heterogeneous resistance landscape.

The p110-beta story should be framed with equal precision. In BYL719-resistant PIK3CA-mutant breast cancer cell models, resistance could occur without obvious PTEN expression loss through an IGF1R/IRS/p85/p110-beta complex that maintained AKT/mTOR/S6K signaling; IGF1R or p110-beta inhibition restored sensitivity in vitro and improved xenograft control [24]. Other work suggests non-genetic PTEN inactivation through USP10/GSK3-beta-mediated phosphorylation and impaired PTEN dimerization in resistant breast cancer models and patient-derived organoids [25]. Together these studies support p110-beta and PTEN-function biology as major resistance routes, but the assay used to define PTEN status matters. Mutation, copy number, IHC, phosphorylation, dimerization, PIP3, pAKT, and pS6 are not interchangeable biomarkers [26].

### 4. Secondary PIK3CA Mutations Define a Distinct On-Target Resistance Class

Secondary PIK3CA mutations deserve their own category because they can represent direct target-site evolution. Serial liquid biopsy and rapid-autopsy analyses of resistance to orthosteric PI3K-alpha inhibitors identified acquired PIK3CA alterations, including W780 and Q859-region mutations, alongside AKT1 and PTEN events [19]. Functional modeling showed that some secondary PIK3CA mutations reduce sensitivity to orthosteric inhibitors while retaining sensitivity to allosteric PI3K-alpha inhibition, whereas co-occurring downstream alterations may require a different strategy [19]. This is the strongest basis for class-switch thinking after orthosteric PI3K-alpha inhibitor resistance.

That claim should not be blurred with baseline multiple PIK3CA mutations. In SANDPIPER analyses, clonality of multiple PIK3CA mutations correlated with outcomes on taselisib plus fulvestrant, suggesting that some double-mutant tumors may be more pathway dependent rather than intrinsically resistant [28]. Broader mutation-spectrum studies likewise show that non-hotspot and multiple PIK3CA mutations are clinically and biologically diverse [2,29]. The practical distinction is temporal: baseline double or noncanonical PIK3CA mutation can inform eligibility and pathway dependence, whereas acquired secondary PIK3CA mutation after exposure can indicate on-target resistance.

The downstream consequence is that serial profiling matters. A single archival tumor sample cannot distinguish a truncal activating mutation from a later resistance mutation, and ctDNA timing may determine whether a resistance allele is detectable before radiographic progression. The same patient may harbor secondary PIK3CA mutation, AKT1 activation, PTEN loss, and RTK/MAPK bypass at different sites or time points [19,23]. Treatment selection after progression should therefore avoid reducing resistance to “PIK3CA positive” versus “PIK3CA negative”; it should ask whether the new alteration changes inhibitor binding, restores pathway output downstream, or points away from PI3K-alpha as the dominant dependency.

### 5. Persistent AKT-mTOR Output Is a Phenotype With Multiple Causes

Several resistance mechanisms converge on a shared pharmacodynamic endpoint: persistent mTORC1/S6 output despite PI3K-alpha inhibition. In PIK3CA-mutant breast cancer models, sensitivity to BYL719 required durable suppression of mTORC1, whereas resistant models showed persistent or rebounding pS6 even when proximal Akt phosphorylation was inhibited; adding mTORC1 inhibition restored sensitivity in experimental systems [30]. Acquired BYL719-resistant breast cancer cells can also maintain AKT/mTOR/S6K signaling through IGF1R-p110-beta coupling [24]. These studies make pS6 and related readouts useful as pathway-output markers, but they do not by themselves identify the upstream cause.

Other mechanisms can produce the same output. Functional resistance screens implicated PIM-family signaling and other pathways in resistance to PI3K inhibition, with paired biopsy observations suggesting clinical relevance but limited sample size [27]. In head and neck and esophageal squamous carcinoma models, AXL-EGFR-PLCgamma-PKC signaling sustained mTOR activity under PI3K-alpha inhibition, and combined blockade of the matched bypass nodes restored sensitivity [31]. In HRAS- or PIK3CA-altered HNSCC, adding tipifarnib made PI3K-alpha pathway suppression more durable in models and early clinical observations by affecting HRAS and RHEB-related signaling [32]. These studies argue for measuring timing and rescue logic: pathway rebound at 24 hours, persistent pS6 after chronic exposure, and restored sensitivity with an RTK, p110-beta, MEK, PKC, PIM, or mTOR-directed combination point to different mechanisms.

Cell state adds another layer. CD44-hyaluronan signaling, receptor tyrosine kinase integration, FGFR1 or FGFR2 context, NF1 loss, IGF2/IGF1R signaling, and metabolic vulnerabilities have each been linked to PI3K-alpha inhibitor response or resistance in specific models [18,33-38,47,48]. These findings are valuable for mechanism generation and combination design, but they are not interchangeable with patient-level resistance biomarkers. Tumor lineage, endocrine context, HER2 status, squamous versus breast lineage, organoid versus cell-line assay, and drug concentration all affect interpretation.

### 6. Endocrine and Cell-Cycle Context Can Masquerade as PI3K Escape

In hormone receptor-positive breast cancer, PI3K-alpha inhibitors are usually used with endocrine therapy and often after CDK4/6 inhibitors. That makes resistance to the regimen a composite endpoint. The alpelisib plus aromatase inhibitor translational study is especially instructive because it found both PTEN loss and ESR1 mutation expansion among resistant cases [8]. The first suggests PI3K-pathway escape; the second suggests failure of the endocrine partner. BYLieve subgroup analyses and reviews of post-CDK4/6 sequencing similarly show that ESR1, PTEN, AKT, FGFR, KRAS, TP53, RB1, and clinical line of therapy may influence outcomes in ways that are difficult to isolate without prospective sampling and appropriate comparators [26,40-45,51-55].

The clinical implication is that combinations should be described by their intended problem. Fulvestrant or oral SERD strategies address endocrine escape; CDK4/6 partners address cell-cycle dependence; AKT, mTOR, p110-beta, FGFR, IGF1R, AXL, EGFR, or MEK combinations address different pathway or bypass hypotheses [31,35-38,43,51-55]. Sequential use of alpelisib, everolimus, or AKT-pathway agents is clinically plausible in selected contexts, but the evidence base is heterogeneous and often retrospective or review-level [43,44,51,52]. A careful review should therefore avoid presenting a combination as “overcoming resistance” unless the study actually tested a resistant state, measured the proposed mechanism, and showed rescue or improved clinical outcome in the relevant population.

### 7. A Practical Evidence Hierarchy for Resistance Claims

The most reliable resistance claims combine three elements: treatment-timed patient material, a mechanism that matches the assay, and functional evidence that perturbing the mechanism changes drug response. PTEN loss after BYL719 progression plus p110-beta rescue is a strong example [23]. Acquired secondary PIK3CA mutations after orthosteric inhibitor exposure plus allele-specific modeling are another [19]. Trial-translational evidence linking PTEN and ESR1 alterations to alpelisib plus aromatase inhibitor resistance is clinically important but must be interpreted as combination-regimen resistance rather than pure PI3K-alpha escape [8].

A second tier contains robust model-supported mechanisms with less direct patient progression evidence: IGF1R-p110-beta compensation, AXL/EGFR/PKC bypass, TAM/MAPK changes, FGFR context, CD44-hyaluronan signaling, NF1 loss, IGF2 signaling, and metabolic dependencies [18,24,25,31,33-38,47,48]. These mechanisms are credible and useful for designing studies, especially when they include rescue experiments, but they need validation in treatment-timed specimens and clinically feasible combinations. A third tier includes reviews, retrospective real-world series, and biomarker frameworks [26,40-45,49-55]. These sources are valuable for clinical context and hypothesis prioritization, but they should not be used as primary proof of a specific resistance mechanism.

Biomarker claims should be classified along three axes. First, timing: baseline eligibility, early adaptive response, or acquired progression after benefit. Second, specimen: archival primary tumor, metastatic tissue, ctDNA, rapid autopsy, organoid, xenograft, or pharmacodynamic biopsy. Third, action: eligibility for PI3K-alpha inhibition, endocrine-partner switching, AKT/mTOR pathway targeting, RTK or MAPK combination, allosteric PI3K-alpha class switching, metabolic management, or investigational follow-up. This classification prevents a common error: treating any pathway alteration detected in a progressing tumor as proof that a matching drug will work.

### 8. Conclusions and Priorities

Resistance to PI3K-alpha inhibition is best understood as a set of overlapping selective pressures rather than a single linear pathway. PIK3CA mutation defines access to therapy in established settings, but resistance may involve insufficient exposure, endocrine escape, PTEN loss, p110-beta compensation, secondary PIK3CA mutations, AKT/mTOR persistence, RTK/MAPK bypass, and tumor-state plasticity. The strongest evidence supports PTEN-linked escape and secondary PIK3CA on-target resistance as clinically observed mechanisms, while many bypass and state mechanisms remain compelling but model-dependent.

Future studies should pair serial ctDNA with tissue or organoid sampling where feasible; report drug exposure, dose interruptions, and metabolic toxicity alongside molecular evolution; distinguish endocrine-partner escape from PI3K-pathway escape; and test rescue strategies in models that match the patient mechanism. Claims about class switching to allosteric PI3K-alpha inhibitors should be restricted to the secondary PIK3CA contexts where that logic has been directly supported. Claims about p110-beta, RTK, MAPK, mTOR, FGFR, IGF1R, CD44, NF1, or metabolic interventions should name the model system and validation level. The useful clinical posture is not to search for one dominant resistance mechanism, but to build a timed, assay-aware resistance diagnosis that can separate established actionability from rational experimentation.
